# B.O.B. — Laissez Faire and the Son of the Sea

B.O.B.'s origin in two chapters. **I — Laissez Faire:** a drunk, murderous thief on Basilisk Point dies in the gutter after saving the scholar Elara and her baby from bandits, begging for one more chance. **II — The Son of the Sea:** Bob, raised by Elara, pulls a half-drowned [[Ronan]] from the shore; Ronan works at the Scholars' Guild until bounty hunters come for him, and Bob's attempt to protect him with an experimental core costs Bob an arm and a leg and fuses the core into his chest. Elara forces Ronan to leave; he leaves Bob a note to find him once he is out on the sea. Originally the root note `Bob Backstory.md`.

## **Chapter I — Laissez Faire**

Laissez Faire had long since forgotten what sobriety felt like.

The world came to him in fragments now—broken light through broken teeth, laughter rotting into echoes, the taste of iron and cheap liquor forever clinging to his tongue. His days blurred together in the gutters of Basilisk Point’s lower quarter, where men like him slept when the taverns spat them back out and woke when hunger or pain demanded attention.

He stole.  
He killed when stealing wasn’t enough.  
He pillaged whatever didn’t fight back hard enough.

It wasn’t cruelty anymore. Just routine.

Once, long ago, he had cared. That part of him died the night his wife did—her breath rattling, skin burning, eyes wide with terror as the sickness ate her from the inside out. The doctor had shaken his head before Laissez Faire even finished begging.

Incurable.

The child inside her never took a breath.

He knew why.  
He always knew.

He’d brought the sickness home with him—some nameless filth picked up during a raid, sweat and blood exchanged for coin. By the time the fever took her, remorse had already hollowed him out.

Since then, nothing stuck. No guilt. No hope. Just drink.

So when the noise dragged him half-awake—shouting, laughter, the scrape of boots on stone—his first thought was irritation.

_Gods, my head…_  
_What’s all this damn commotion…_

He cracked one eye open from where he lay slumped beside a tavern wall. The street swam. Shapes resolved into figures—four men, maybe five. Bandits by the look of them. Drunk. Armed poorly, which made them dangerous in a way professionals never were.

They had someone cornered.

A woman.

Clothes too clean for this side of town. Posture too straight. One of _those_ people—scholars, researchers, fools who thought knowledge insulated them from knives.

_Idiot,_ he thought distantly.  
_They should know better by now._

Not his problem.

He turned his face away—

—and froze.

A sound cut through the fog in his skull.

Crying.

Thin. Fragile. Desperate.

A baby.

Something inside his chest twisted violently, like a wound reopening that never truly healed. The sound reached past the alcohol, past the rot, straight into a place he thought long dead.

His wife’s face flickered behind his eyes.  
Her hand on her belly.  
The promise of something he never got to ruin.

Across the street, the woman clutched a bundled shape tight to her chest.

---

Elara backed away until stone pressed cold against her spine.

“Please,” she said, forcing steadiness into her voice. “Just let us pass. I have nothing to give you.”

One of the bandits laughed, words slurring together. He leaned closer, breath sour with drink.

“Oh, surely you do,” he said, eyes dropping to the bundle. “Look at that thing you’re carrying. Bet it’d fetch us a fine deal.”

Her grip tightened. The baby whimpered.

Panic clawed up her throat.

Then—

_CRASH._

A barrel flew out of nowhere and smashed into the drunk’s side, knocking him flat with a wet, cracking sound.

Elara turned.

Laissez Faire stood swaying in the street, bloodshot eyes half-lidded, bottle dangling loosely from his fingers. He looked like death that had forgotten to finish the job.

“Hey,” he slurred. “Sons of bitches… keep it down, would ya… _hic_.”

She recognized him instantly.

Everyone did.

The lowlife who haunted the gutters. The murderer. The drunk.

He met her gaze for half a second and jerked his head sharply to the side.

_Run._

Elara didn’t hesitate.

She bolted.

Behind her, shouting erupted—rage, confusion, boots scrambling. She ducked into an alley and pressed herself against the wall, heart hammering so loud she was sure they’d hear it.

They didn’t chase her.

They chased him.

The sounds that followed were not quick.

Fists.  
Wood cracking against bone.  
A scream cut short.

Elara covered her mouth, tears burning her eyes. Every instinct screamed at her to go back—but she didn’t. She ran until the cries were swallowed by distance and fear carried her forward.

---

Laissez Faire lay face-down in the mud, barely breathing.

They had done a thorough job. His throat was crushed. Every breath was a knife scraping against collapsing lungs. Blood bubbled weakly from his mouth, pink foam mixing with rainwater and filth.

The world dimmed at the edges.

Funny, he thought distantly.  
_Bubbles._

His vision blurred. Pain faded into something soft and heavy.

For the first time in years, his thoughts were clear.

_So this is it,_ he realized.  
_Figures._

Faces drifted through his mind—his wife, pale and smiling the way she had before the sickness. A child that never lived. A future he never deserved.

His chest convulsed.

With what little strength remained, he tried to draw one last breath.

_Give me one more chance,_ he begged silently, to no god in particular.  
_Maybe… I’ll be a better person this time around._

The rain kept falling.

And Laissez Faire, thief, killer, drunk, died in the gutter—his last act buying a future he would never see.

## **Chapter II — The Son of the Sea**

Bob went to the shore at dawn because it hurt less that way.

The mornings on Basilisk Point were quiet enough to think. The sea breathed slowly against the fossil cliffs, waves breaking with a patience that felt ancient. Bob walked the familiar path barefoot, a satchel slung over one shoulder, salt stinging the old cuts on his hands. Elara’s instruments—abandoned now, half-rusted from disuse—still needed tending. Salt crept into lenses and joints if left unchecked, and Bob could not bear to let them decay.

They were all he had left of her work.

He knelt beside a half-buried tripod when he noticed the shape by the waterline.

At first, he thought it was driftwood.

Then the driftwood breathed.

The man lay sprawled where the tide had given him up, skin pale, clothes torn, blood drying black along his ribs. Each breath came shallow and uneven, as if the sea itself had loaned him air reluctantly.

Bob’s heart hammered.

“Hey—hey!” he said, dropping the satchel and sliding through wet sand. He pressed two fingers to the man’s neck. A pulse. Weak, but there.

Bob didn’t think. He acted.

He rolled the stranger onto his side, cleared his airway, pressed rhythm into the man’s chest the way Elara had taught him—_steady, not frantic, science doesn’t respond to panic._ When the man coughed and seawater spilled from his mouth, Bob nearly laughed with relief.

“Don’t die,” he muttered. “I just fixed you.”

The man didn’t answer.

Bob hauled him onto his shoulders—heavier than he looked—and staggered back toward home.

---

Elara took over without a word.

She cut away ruined cloth, cleaned wounds, stitched flesh with hands that did not tremble. The man drifted in and out of consciousness for days, fever burning through him like something hunted and cornered.

When he finally woke, he did so violently.

Bob was at the doorway when the stranger bolted upright, knocking over a chair, gasping like he expected steel at his throat. Bob froze.

“It’s fine,” Bob said quickly. “You’re safe. You’re—”

The man’s eyes locked onto him.

Recognition flared there. Sharp. Immediate.

“You,” he rasped. Then, quieter: “You pulled me out.”

Bob nodded.

The man exhaled slowly, like he’d been holding his breath since he washed ashore. He swung his legs off the bed, wincing, and stood—unsteady, but standing.

“My name’s Ronan,” he said. “I owe you my life. Tell me how I repay it.”

As he pulled on his shirt, Bob noticed the scars.

Knuckles split and healed a hundred times over. Old lash marks across his back. Deep, puckered wounds along his ribs that no animal could have made. This wasn’t the body of a scholar or sailor.

This was the body of someone who survived violence for a living.

Bob hesitated. “You don’t… have to repay anything.”

Ronan snorted softly. “Everyone pays eventually.”

He glanced around the house—charts, instruments, half-finished devices scattered across tables. “I’m not good with the smart stuff you people do here,” he said. “But if you need a workhorse, I can lift, dig, guard. I need a job anyway. I’ve got to get to the North Blue. That takes berries.”

Bob pointed him toward the Scholars’ Guild.

Ronan went.

And then he kept coming back.

---

Ronan helped with repairs, hauled equipment, ran errands Elara could no longer manage alone. He spoke little, smiled less, but when Bob showed him some half-assembled contraption—a grappling launcher that jammed constantly, a compression valve that blew seals—Ronan watched with quiet interest.

“That one’ll kill you,” he said once, nodding at a sparking core.

Bob grinned. “Only if I’m wrong.”

That earned a short, surprised chuckle.

It was the first time Bob realized something terrifying.

He had made a friend.

One evening, Bob reached for Ronan’s bag without thinking—curiosity overriding caution. Ronan’s reaction was instant. He grabbed the strap, eyes hard, grip iron-strong.

“Don’t,” he said.

Bob recoiled. “Sorry. I didn’t—”

Ronan hesitated, then sighed. He opened the bag just enough to show what lay within.

A fruit.

Wrong in every way. Swirling patterns etched into its skin, colors too vivid, too deliberate. It felt like looking at a lie pretending to be food.

“A Devil Fruit,” Ronan said quietly.

Bob stared.

He’d heard the stories. Everyone had. Men who burned without flame. Women who vanished into smoke. Powers with no equations, no repeatable logic.

“That’s not… possible,” Bob whispered. “There’s no mechanism. No law—”

Ronan smiled faintly. “And yet.”

Bob couldn’t sleep that night.

---

The mercenaries came two days later.

Bob was returning from a nearby town with supplies when they stepped into his path—four of them, armed, professional. Not bandits. Hunters.

They asked for a description. A man who washed ashore. Dangerous. Wanted.

Bob said he didn’t recognize him.

One of them watched him too closely.

They followed him back.

Ronan sensed them before they reached the house.

“Take me,” Ronan said calmly when they confronted him. “Leave them out of it.”

Elara protested. Bob shook his head, panic rising.

Ronan was already walking.

That night, Bob followed.

He stayed hidden as the mercenaries dragged Ronan toward the shore, ropes biting into his wrists. The sea loomed dark and endless behind them.

At the waterline, Ronan snapped the ropes like twine.

The fight was brutal and fast.

Ronan moved like a force of nature—his fists landed with the weight of boulders. One man flew into the sea and did not surface. He didn’t thrash. He didn’t swim.

It was as if the ocean had claimed him.

Then a spear burst through Ronan’s back.

The man holding it flickered—his body dissolving into mirage before reforming. A Devil Fruit user.

Ronan screamed.

Bob’s hands shook as he pulled the core from his satchel.

Elara’s voice echoed in his head: _Don’t get hurt. Don’t fight._

He ignored it.

He strapped the core to his chest, poured salt into the chamber, locked the gauntlet onto his arm. The device whined as pressure built—too much, too fast.

Ronan saw him then.

“Bob,” he shouted. “Stop! That thing—”

The spearman lunged.

Bob pulled the trigger.

The explosion tore the night open.

---

Bob woke to pain and screaming.

His own—or Elara’s—he couldn’t tell.

One arm was gone. One leg shattered beyond saving. The core was fused into his chest, metal and flesh indistinguishable. Elara had done the impossible to keep him alive.

Ronan was gone.

All Bob had was a note.

_Bob. Find me once you are out on the sea. I am sorry—but I am glad you tried to protect me._

Later, Bob learned the rest.

Elara had forced Ronan to leave. Never return. His last words to her had been quiet, resolute:

“Please do not keep him here. He has the potential to be strong—only if you allow it.”

When Bob recovered enough to stand, Elara held his face in her hands, tears streaking freely.

“You are all I have,” she said. “Protect yourself. If you die… I will not survive you.”

Bob nodded.

And somewhere deep inside him, the sea stirred—calling, patient, inevitable.
